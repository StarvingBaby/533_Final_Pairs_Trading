Overview

We run a pairs trading strategy across three energy sector pairs simultaneously, capturing relative value dislocations within the energy industry.

The three pairs span different parts of the energy value chain — all driven by the same macro environment (oil prices, energy demand, capex cycles) but reacting differently in the short term, creating tradeable spreads.

Pair Sub-sector Economic link
SLB / HAL Oilfield Services Both serve upstream drillers; revenue tracks rig counts
PSX / VLO Refining Both earn on crack spread; same input/output
XOM / CVX Integrated Majors Largest US oil majors; same macro exposure

Formation period: 2024-03-01 → 2025-03-01
Backtest period: 2025-03-03 → 2026-05-01
Capital: $100,000 split equally across 3 pairs (~$33,333 each)

Pair Selection & Rationale

All three pairs share a core thesis: companies in the same sub-sector of energy are exposed to the same macro drivers, so short-term price dislocations between them tend to revert.

SLB / HAL — Schlumberger and Halliburton are the two largest oilfield services companies globally. Both provide drilling, completion, and reservoir services to the same customers. When one temporarily outperforms the other, competitive dynamics pull the spread back.

PSX / VLO — Phillips 66 and Valero are pure-play refiners. Their margins are entirely driven by the crack spread — the difference between crude oil input cost and refined product prices. Same input, same output, same margin driver.

XOM / CVX — ExxonMobil and Chevron are the two largest US integrated oil majors. Both produce, refine, and sell oil and gas globally. Their relative valuation is driven by capital allocation and dividend policy, making the spread mean-reverting over medium horizons.

Strategy Design

Rolling OLS - Dynamic Hedge Ratio

Instead of fixing the hedge ratio at the formation-period OLS estimate, we use a 60-day rolling OLS window that updates every trading day. This lets the strategy adapt as the relative valuation between the two companies shifts over time.

Entry Rules

  • Long spread (long A, short B): z-score drops below -2.0
  • Short spread (short A, long B): z-score rises above +2.0

We chose ±2.0 because it is wide enough to filter out daily noise but still occurs frequently enough to generate meaningful trades over a year-long backtest.

Exit Rules

Fate Trigger Interpretation
Mean reversion z-score crosses 0 Spread returned to equilibrium
Stop-loss |z-score| > 4.0 Spread kept moving against us - cut loss
End of backtest Position open at end Force-closed at last price

Position Sizing

  • ~$33,333 allocated per pair (dollar-neutral: ~$16,667 per leg)
  • 5 bps transaction cost one-way (10 bps round-trip per trade)

Discussion

What happened

Every trade was long spread - all 6 entries happened when z-score dropped below -2.0, none when it rose above +2.0. For a mean-reversion strategy, that’s a red flag. It suggests the spread wasn’t randomly oscillating around its historical mean; it was systematically below it for most of the backtest period.

SLB/HAL was the main drag, losing 2 of 3 trades for a total return of -5.29%. Trade 4 was the worst - entered at z = -2.24 in September 2025, but the spread kept falling before finally recovering 30 days later, costing -3.97%. The rolling hedge ratio for SLB/HAL drifted well above its formation estimate of 0.668 during mid-2025, which distorted the z-score and made entry signals unreliable.

PSX/VLO was the best of the three, finishing +0.99% with a 66.7% win rate. Its ADF result was the strongest at p = 0.048, and that held up - the spread behaved more cleanly during the backtest.

XOM/CVX generated zero trades. The z-score never crossed ±2.0, which actually makes sense given its weak formation-period statistics (ADF p = 0.132). Including it added no value in this backtest.

The one genuinely positive sign: all 6 trades exited via mean reversion, zero stop-losses. The spread always came back eventually - we just weren’t always on the right side of the initial move.

Risks

All three pairs are in the same sector, so a broad energy selloff would hit all three spreads at once - there’s no cross-sector cushion. XOM/CVX failed both statistical tests in the formation period and probably shouldn’t have been included. The 6-trade sample is also too small to say much statistically; the results could easily look different over a longer backtest window.

What we’d do differently

Adding pairs from outside energy would be the most impactful change - financial or consumer pairs would reduce the sector concentration risk. Beyond that, a regime filter like VIX > 25 would help avoid entering during high-volatility periods when spreads tend to trend rather than revert. On the exit side, partial takes at z = 0.5 would lock in gains faster instead of waiting for a full reversion to 0.

Strategy Monitoring

How will we know the strategy is performing as expected?

We track three metrics on a rolling 30-day basis after going live:

  1. Rolling Sharpe ratio - if it drops below -0.5 (our backtest benchmark), the strategy is underperforming. We give it one full month before making any changes to avoid reacting to noise.

  2. Z-score crossing rate - if the spread goes more than 60 consecutive days without crossing 0, it’s trending rather than mean-reverting.

  3. Trade direction balance - if more than 80% of trades over a 3-month window are in the same direction, that signals systematic drift rather than random mean reversion.

How will we quantify when the strategy stops working?

Two hard stop conditions:

  1. Stop-loss rate exceeds 50% over any rolling 10-trade window - the spread is consistently moving against us.

  2. Rolling hedge ratio drifts more than 40% from the formation-period estimate - for example, if SLB/HAL’s hedge ratio was 0.668 at formation and moves above 0.935 or below 0.401, we treat the relationship as structurally changed.

If either condition is triggered, we stop trading, re-run the screener on a fresh 12-month formation window, and re-validate before resuming.
